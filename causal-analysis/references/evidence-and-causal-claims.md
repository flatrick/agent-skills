# Evidence and causal claims

## Claim types

- A trigger initiates or exposes the sequence in scope.
- A proximate cause directly produces or permits the outcome.
- A contributing factor changes the likelihood or severity but is not sufficient by itself.
- A condition is relevant context without a demonstrated causal link.
- A failed control is a missing, failed, bypassed, or ineffective safeguard.
- An unresolved hypothesis is plausible but not distinguished by current evidence.

These roles depend on the defined outcome and scope.
The same event can have a different role in another analysis.

## Support a causal link

For a claim that A contributed to B, record:

1. Evidence that A and B occurred, with provenance and time bounds.
2. A mechanism by which A could change B.
3. Evidence of the expected association, sequence, state change, or intervention.
4. Counterexamples and alternative explanations considered.
5. A safe counterfactual test when one is possible: if A were absent or changed, what observable result should differ?
6. The scope and uncertainty of the claim.

Temporal order is necessary for many causal claims but is not sufficient.
A plausible mechanism is not evidence that the mechanism operated in this event.
A successful corrective action can strengthen a claim, but only if the test separates its effect from concurrent changes.

When evidence is exhausted, say `unresolved` or `undetermined`.
Do not fill gaps with confidence.

## Human and organizational evidence

Evaluate what information was available at the time, not what became known later.
Examine workload, time pressure, interface design, competing goals, procedures, training, staffing, incentives, communication, supervision, and control design.
Corroborate interviews and recollections with records, independent accounts, physical evidence, or system data where possible.
Record conflicts without forcing consensus.

Accountability and system context can coexist.
State which action occurred, which expectation applied, what made the action more or less likely, and which controls should have detected or contained it.
